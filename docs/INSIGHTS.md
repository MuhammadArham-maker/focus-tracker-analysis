# Insights & Findings

These are the patterns I noticed while exploring my own 79 records. I separate the counts from my interpretation because the spreadsheet can show what I recorded, but it cannot fully explain why a session felt the way it did.

## Dataset snapshot

- 79 self-recorded sessions, submitted between 12 and 22 May 2026 across 9 dates.
- Average focus score: **3.19 / 5**.
- The score 3 was most common: 44 sessions (55.7%).
- Scores 3 and 4 together accounted for 67 sessions (84.8%).

| Score | Sessions | Share |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 2 | 2.5% |
| 2 | 8 | 10.1% |
| 3 | 44 | 55.7% |
| 4 | 23 | 29.1% |
| 5 | 2 | 2.5% |

## 1. Movement & Creation — looking at sessions without Overthinking

Overthinking was selected in 71 of 79 sessions (89.9%). Instead of looking at the 71 records where it appeared, I filtered for the eight where I did **not** select it.

| Activity | Sessions |
|---|---:|
| Calisthenics | 3 |
| Talk / Social Connection | 2 |
| Unstructured Screen Time | 2 |
| YouTube Content Creation | 1 |

The eight scores were: four 3s, two 4s, and two 5s.

This made me curious about physical movement, social interaction, and creative activity. It did not prove that these activities remove Overthinking; it gave me a smaller group of sessions to reflect on.

## 2. The Flow Assassin — when Natural Flow was absent

There were five sessions where I did not select Natural Flow.

- All five included Overthinking.
- All five had a focus score of 1 or 2 (two 1s and three 2s).
- Three also included Mental Resistance.
- Three included Extreme emotions.
- Three included Good Environment.

The Good Environment entries stood out to me. In three of these low-scoring sessions, I still described the environment as good.

My note from this filter was:

> A quiet room cannot fix a loud mind.

That is my interpretation of these five records, not a universal psychological rule. It made me look more closely at mental resistance, emotional carryover, and Overthinking rather than assuming the room was always the problem.

## 3. The Anatomy of a 4 — and how rare a 5 was

I initially wanted to find the recipe for a perfect score of 5. The workbook contains only **two** score-5 sessions, so I cannot make a reliable general rule from them.

Both score-5 sessions included:
- Natural Flow.
- Self Talk (Bismillah...).

The two activities were YouTube Content Creation and Talk / Social Connection. Both were recorded in the Afternoon time block.

I then looked at the 23 score-4 sessions:

| Driver | Score-4 sessions selecting it |
|---|---:|
| Natural Flow | 23 of 23 |
| Overthinking | 21 of 23 |
| Self Talk (Bismillah...) | 18 of 23 |
| Hyper-Clarity | 15 of 23 |

This changed the question for me. Overthinking could still be present in a session I rated 4. I began thinking less about eliminating every thought and more about returning to the action in front of me.

## 4. The Golden Hour — time block comparison

I explored whether a particular time of day produced better focus.

| Time block | Sessions | Average score |
|---|---:|---:|
| Pre-Dawn / Early Morning | 14 | 3.07 |
| Late Morning | 20 | 3.10 |
| Afternoon | 20 | 3.30 |
| Evening | 23 | 3.26 |
| Night | 2 | 3.00 |

The averages were fairly close in this sample. Night had only two records. I did not find a strong time-of-day pattern in these observations.

This pushed me to consider whether the activity and my level of engagement mattered more than the clock. For example, reading Quran or listening to Hadees / Muzakra could require active attention and novelty for me. That is a reflection from my experience, not something these averages prove.

## 5. Activity averages — the importance of context

The activity summary from the row-level workbook is:

| Activity | Sessions | Average score |
|---|---:|---:|
| Namaz | 32 | 2.94 |
| Quran | 11 | 3.64 |
| Hadees / Muzakra | 4 | 3.50 |
| Football | 4 | 3.00 |
| Calisthenics | 3 | 3.33 |
| Active Building (Projects) | 2 | 3.00 |
| Passive Learning (Coursera / Tutorials) | 1 | 4.00 |
| University Passive Learning (Exam Lectures) | 8 | 3.25 |
| YouTube Content Creation | 3 | 3.67 |
| Talk / Social Connection | 8 | 3.25 |
| Unstructured Screen Time | 3 | 3.00 |

Martial Arts and University Active Practical had no recorded sessions.

I had to be careful with the averages. The 4.00 for Personal Passive Learning came from one session. The Talk / Social Connection average of 3.25 also hid a wide spread of scores from 2 to 5.

I also questioned the comparison between university exam lectures (3.25 across eight records) and personal passive learning (4.00 from one). The records mostly captured a period when I was switching between studying for university exams and trying to understand topics more deeply. I did not record enough of the later, more exam-aligned period to make a fair comparison.

I revisited one session that had been labelled as passive learning. In reality, I had gone beyond watching a course: I explored an idea, used AI to brainstorm, looked up shadow techniques, and drew a cracked coffee mug with a chain handle to represent struggle.

That made me question the label. The activity choice did not fully capture the creative work inside that session.

My interpretation shifted toward **pure theory versus real-world application and creation**. That is a contextual reading of my records, not a result the spreadsheet can establish on its own.

## 6. The Autopilot Trap

Namaz had 32 recorded sessions and an average focus score of 2.94. That number raised a question for me because the activity is familiar and repeated.

I started thinking about how familiar actions can become automatic. My reflection was that a task does not always need to change; sometimes I need to change how deliberately I attend to it.

I called this the **Autopilot Trap**: relying only on external novelty or an interesting activity may not be enough, because even interesting things become familiar through repetition.

This was a personal interpretation built from the numbers and my experience. The activity average does not prove that familiarity caused the lower score.

## 7. What I took away

The data made me look again at my first assumptions:

- A good environment did not always line up with a high score.
- Overthinking appeared in many sessions, including most score-4 sessions.
- Time-block averages were close in this sample.
- Averages hid differences between individual sessions.
- Activity labels sometimes missed the context of what I was actually doing.

The spreadsheet helped me see patterns. My own experience helped me question what those patterns meant.

My personal takeaway became:

> **Focus in me & my moment.**

This is what I took from my own experiment, not a universal prescription for anyone else's focus.
